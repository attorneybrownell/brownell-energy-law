"The Granite Peak PPA in eight minutes"

In a paid engagement this ships as a produced audio file — two voices, ~8 minutes — for the drive to the meeting. The full transcript is shown here so you can judge the substance.

Host

So Northline just got the draft power purchase agreement back from Granite Peak for the Meridian Ridge project. Sixty-two pages. If you're on the board and you've got eight minutes — what do you actually need to know?

Counsel

Three things, honestly. The price is fine, the paper is not, and everything wrong with it is fixable. That's the whole review in one sentence.

Host

Start with the price being fine, because that's the part everyone asks first.

Counsel

Seven point eight five cents a kilowatt-hour, escalating one and a half percent a year, for twenty-five years. For a four-megawatt ground-mount in that market, that's a solid deal. Nobody should walk away from this price. Which is exactly why the contract terms matter so much — there's real value here worth protecting.

Host

Okay. So what's wrong with the paper?

Counsel

The big one is section 11.2. Granite Peak can terminate the contract at any time, for any reason, with six months' notice — and all they owe is the quote-unquote unamortized capital cost of the system. Think about what that means. Year six, they decide they don't want the deal anymore. They write a check for what's left on the equipment, and they walk. Your lost profit for the remaining nineteen years? Gone. The tax credits that can get clawed back? Your problem. The loan you have to unwind early? Also your problem.

Host

And I'm guessing the people financing the project have opinions about that.

Counsel

That's the real point. Forget whether it's unfair — it's unfinanceable. A tax equity investor is buying a twenty-five-year revenue stream. If the other side can switch that stream off at book value, there's nothing to invest in. So the ask is simple: delete the termination right, or price it honestly with a termination value schedule — an exhibit that says, year by year, here's the check that actually makes everyone whole.

Host

You said three things are wrong. What's number two?

Counsel

The assignment clause — 14.1. As drafted, Northline can't assign the contract to anyone, including its own lenders as collateral, without Granite Peak's consent. In their sole discretion. Here's the thing though: this is the most commonly conceded fix in the entire industry. Every offtaker's first draft says this, and every financeable PPA ends up with carve-outs for lenders, tax equity, and affiliates. It's severe and easy to fix at the same time — which is why it goes in the very first counterproposal, framed not as a favor but as table stakes.

Host

And number three?

Counsel

Look at who's actually signing. Not Granite Peak Beverage — a subsidiary called GPB Operations LLC. No credit rating, no disclosed balance sheet, and the draft offers no guaranty from the parent. So the twenty-five-year revenue stream we keep talking about is only as good as a shell company's promise. The ask: parent guaranty, or a letter of credit sized around a year of revenue. This one will be a genuine fight — companies hate signing guaranties — but it's the fight worth having, because the lenders will demand it anyway.

Host

Those are the three blockers. What's in the rest of the stack?

Counsel

Seven more issues, mostly the usual suspects. An output guarantee with unlimited damages and no allowance for the fact that panels degrade — that needs a cap and a degradation curve. Delay damages that start on day one, while the two most common causes of delay, equipment deliveries and the utility's own interconnection timeline, are excluded from force majeure. A purchase option where Granite Peak's own appraiser decides what the project is worth — that becomes the standard three-appraiser process. Free unlimited curtailment, a one-way indemnity, oversized insurance requirements, and all change-of-law risk parked on the seller. Each one has a market-standard fix.

Host

So how does the negotiation actually play?

Counsel

Three rounds, and sequencing is the strategy. Round one is the financeability package — termination, assignment, credit support — presented as what any bankable PPA requires. Round two, we hold the line on the damages cap, and we pay for it with concessions that cost Granite Peak nothing to accept, like the appraisal procedure and mutual indemnities. Round three, we give ground on insurance limits — the cheapest thing on our list — in exchange for whatever's still open on credit support. The walk-away line is bright: no financing carve-outs or no real credit support means no deal, because there's no project without the financing.

Host

Bottom line for the board?

Counsel

Good deal, wrong paper, clear path. Don't sign the draft. Authorize the counterproposal, expect about six weeks to execution, and know exactly which two points we'd walk over. Everything else is negotiation, not risk.
